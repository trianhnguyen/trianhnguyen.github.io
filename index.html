Doctoral Candidate · Institute for Environmental Economics and World Trade

I am a doctoral candidate and a junior researcher working at the Institute for Environmental Economics and World Trade, Leibniz Universität Hannover. I am an applied economist, with focus on crime, environmental economics, and economics of culture.

My research examines how environmental and demographic shocks reshape conflict, crime, and economic outcomes in Thailand and Vietnam. Precisely, I study how extreme weather, return migration, urbanization, and trade agreements affect households' welfare, and which policy can mitigate/strengthen the impacts.

Before joining LUH, I obtained my M.Sc. in Economic Theory and Econometrics from the E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University and Toulouse School of Economics.

Weather Extremes and Social Conflict in Vietnam

My job market paper studies the causal impacts of extreme weather on social conflict in Vietnam.

Show abstract

Climate change threatens developing countries, but credible evidence linking extreme weather to conflict through farm income remains scarce. We isolate the income channel using a triple-difference design and over 80,000 cell-month observations in Vietnam between 2010 and 2024. Extreme drought during the rice harvest raises organized violence in farming communities. We conduct mechanism tests to distinguish whether weather extremes cause conflict by destroying harvests, by raising temperatures that increase aggression, or by creating opportunities for predation during disorder. Our results rule out heat-driven aggression, opportunistic predation, and temporal displacement as alternative explanations to our income channel. Drought-driven conflict rapidly dissipates across space but persists over time. The weather-conflict link weakens where irrigation protects farm income. Our result extends the income channel to a low-conflict setting and suggests stabilizing farm income during drought as a targeted, actionable lever for conflict prevention in agricultural economies.

Return Migration, Crime, and Conflict in Rural Thailand

Nguyen, Tri-Anh Duc, & Nguyen, T. T. (2026). Return migration, crime, and conflict in rural Thailand. The Journal of Development Studies, 62(10), 1725–1742.

Article DOI ↗
Show abstract

While return migration’s positive economic effects are well-documented, its impact on crime and conflict remains understudied. We examine return migrant effects on crime and conflict in rural Thailand using panel data from 110 subdistricts across three north-eastern provinces (2010–2022). Employing three-stage least squares with weighted-distance unemployment rates as an instrumental variable, we find significant effects on conflict, specifically business fraud, but no evidence for property crime effects or neighbour conflicts. Business fraud effects increase consistently and persist up to nine months upon return. Our findings suggest that while return migrants contribute to local economies through acquired social, financial, and human capital, positive effects can be severely offset by business fraud without proper reintegration policies.

Spillovers of U.S. Monetary Policy Shocks on Housing Prices in Nine Emerging Economies and Their Stabilizing Capital Controls

Nguyen, T. B., Le, C. V., & Nguyen, Tri-Anh Duc (2026). Spillovers of U.S. monetary policy shocks on housing prices in nine emerging economies and their stabilizing capital controls. International Journal of Housing Markets and Analysis, 19(1), 131–149.

Article DOI ↗
Show abstract

U.S. monetary policy influences housing markets worldwide, yet the literature estimating its spillovers conflate two opposing forces within each Federal Reserve announcement. We decompose Fed policy-rate announcements into pure monetary policy shocks and information shocks following Bu, Rogers, and Wu (2021) and estimate their effects on real house price indices using Jordà (2005) local projections in a panel of nine emerging economies between 2005 and 2019. A pure tightening shock depresses housing prices significantly from three quarters onward, and the contractionary effect grows over six consecutive quarters. In contrast, the information shocks raise housing prices within one quarter as markets update expectations about global activity. The two effects partially offset and reduces the net transmission of U.S. rate changes to emerging-market housing below previously reported estimates. Capital controls implemented by emerging economies attenuate both channels. Countries exercising tighter controls experience significantly smaller spillovers. The pure monetary policy results are robust to alternative shock measures from Jarocinski and Karadi (2020). However, the information effect is shorter-lived under the alternative measures.

Urbanization, Employment and Human Capital Investment in Rural Vietnam

With M.H. Do and T.T. Nguyen · First author

Show abstract

Whether rural households benefit from nearby urbanization through commuting alone matters for structural transformation in developing countries. We combine three waves (2019–2024) of a longitudinal household panel with nighttime-light imagery to measure each household's exposure to nearby urbanization. Urban economic expansion raises off-farm employment, income, and education investment among rural Vietnamese households. The urbanization effects increases in the number of the nearest urban centers. Proxied for the ability to find jobs in urban centers, asset wealth and motorcycle ownership determine who benefits most of the gains. Instrumenting off-farm participation, we find that entering off-farm work substantially raises per-child education spending and triples the education budget share. The households that were spending the least on education gain the most, narrowing gaps in human capital investment across families. Off-farm labor markets linked to nearby urban growth reduce education inequality through local commuting.

ICT Adoption and Risk Perception

With M. Donaldson and T.T. Nguyen

Show abstract

Rural households in developing countries face natural hazards but often lack timely information about them. We estimate how information and communication technology adoption shapes risk perception of floods, droughts, storms, disease, and landslides among rural Vietnamese households. Using a longitudinal household survey data from 2012 to 2022 and a simultaneous three-equation system with Heckman selection and an instrumental variable, we find that ICT adoption raises the probability of reporting any expected risk by a sizable amount across five hazard categories. The effect operates on the extensive margin. ICT moves households from perceiving no risk to perceiving some. It does not shift how high-risk households rate the severity of hazards they already recognize. Controlling for lifetime flood exposure absorbs the ICT effect for floods but not for storms. ICT matters most where households lack direct experience with a hazard.
